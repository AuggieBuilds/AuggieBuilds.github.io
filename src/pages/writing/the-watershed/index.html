The governing question

A city’s long-term strength depends not only on attracting money, but on converting temporary inflows into institutions, infrastructure, skills, ownership, and successor industries before the original source declines.

Money can enter a place through exports, public spending, healthcare reimbursement, private investment, construction, and credit. It can leave through imports, distant ownership, debt service, national financial markets, and people.

Outflow is not automatically bad. Regions need trade, outside capital, specialized products, and diversified investments. The danger is dependence: consuming outside value without retaining enough productive capacity to earn the next inflow.

Section 01

The Frame

A city’s economy is easier to understand if you picture a bathtub. Water flows in through the faucet and out through the drain. The size of the tub matters because accumulated assets and institutions buy time, but the governing question is simpler: what is coming in, what is going out, and which is larger?

Scranton illustrates the problem. Around 1900 it exported anthracite coal and received enormous outside revenue in return. The city had 102,026 residents and ranked thirty-eighth among American cities; the 2020 Census counted 76,328. 1 The city did not disappear when coal declined. It retained buildings, people, schools, hospitals, roads, and civic organizations built while the tub was filling. But once the flow reversed, those stocks had to be maintained from a weaker current.

Money moves through a place rather than simply sitting in it. It enters through wages, exports, contracts, grants, reimbursements, construction, and investment. It leaves through imported goods, distant ownership, debt service, national financial markets, insurance, and migration.

This is not an argument that every outflow is harmful. Trade allows a city to obtain products and expertise it cannot efficiently make itself. Diversified retirement accounts reduce household risk. Outside owners can bring capital and operating skill. The structural question is whether the region retains enough income-producing capacity to keep paying for what it imports.

Section 02

Credit Is Another Inflow

Most money in a modern economy is created when commercial banks make loans. The bank records a loan asset and a matching deposit in the borrower’s account. Repayment destroys the deposit money that the loan created. 2

For this essay, the geographic implication matters more than the accounting sequence. A mortgage finances a local house. A business loan finances equipment, inventory, or a new location. A construction loan moves a project from drawings into payroll and materials. Credit is therefore another pipe through which purchasing power enters a place.

Local knowledge can affect where that pipe points. Community banks and credit unions may understand borrowers, properties, and industries that appear too small or unfamiliar to a national underwriting system. National lenders can offer scale, pricing, technology, and diversification. Neither structure is universally superior. The question is whether viable local projects can obtain capital and whether deposits gathered in a region are ever redeployed there.

Why this section is short

Bank-money creation is important, but the full balance-sheet mechanics are not the central argument. The relevant point is that the geography of lending influences which homes, firms, and projects get built.

Section 03

Public Money and the Shape of the Land

In fiscal year 2024, the federal government collected about $4.9 trillion. Individual income taxes supplied roughly 49 percent, payroll taxes 35 percent, corporate income taxes 11 percent, and other receipts the remainder. 3 Once collected, those dollars enter pooled systems. There is no one-project ledger that traces a particular resident’s tax payment to a particular highway, laboratory, hospital reimbursement, or retirement benefit.

The Central Artery/Tunnel Project—better known as Boston’s Big Dig—replaced a deteriorating elevated highway with an underground expressway, extended I-90 to Logan Airport, built major connections, and enabled parks and public spaces above the old artery. The Federal Highway Administration lists a $14.8 billion cost: $7.0 billion in federal aid and $7.8 billion in state bonds. 4

That does not prove Boston or Massachusetts received more from Washington than its residents paid in federal taxes. The defensible point is narrower: the federal share was financed through a national system, while much of the immediate construction activity, engineering experience, transportation capacity, public space, and associated land value accumulated in metropolitan Boston.

Research spending creates a similar channel. Boston received nearly $2.5 billion across more than 4,000 NIH grants in fiscal year 2024, while MIT reported more than $2.2 billion in sponsored research expenditures in fiscal year 2025. 5 Discoveries may benefit the world, but grant offices, laboratories, trained workers, suppliers, and institutional reputation accumulate where the research is performed.

The bathtub explains balance. The watershed explains path dependence. Repeated flows do not merely fill a place; they alter its ability to receive the next flow.

Section 04

Companies Die; Cities Must Outlive Them

Companies and cities are not the same type of organism. A firm can merge, be acquired, go bankrupt, or disappear from a stock exchange. A city generally persists through repeated economic transformations, even when its population and industries change.

A study of publicly traded North American firms found an approximately ten-year half-life in its dataset, regardless of company age. Half-life does not mean that every company disappears after ten years; exits also include mergers and acquisitions. It does mean that corporate mortality is normal. 6

A city’s long-term strategy therefore cannot assume the indefinite survival of any one employer. The city is the long-lived platform. The company is a temporary economic engine operating on it.

A city that depends on one mature employer should treat the productive years as a period for creating successors.

There are three basic destinations for boom-time surplus:

1 · Extract

Dividends, executive compensation, consumption, or investment elsewhere.

2 · Reinforce the incumbent

Modernize or expand the existing company. This may extend its life while deepening dependence on the same system.

3 · Build independent successors

Universities, technical schools, suppliers, research capacity, local funds, infrastructure, and new export businesses that can survive the parent.

The third category is industrial succession. A successor passes an independence test: can it operate if the parent closes; attract its own revenue; serve other customers; finance itself separately; and create transferable capabilities?

What this section addsCorporate reinvestment and city-level succession are different. The latter creates engines that no longer require the parent company.

Section 05

Pittsburgh and Bethlehem

Pittsburgh and Bethlehem were both expressions of the American steel economy. Both suffered severe industrial decline. They were not a controlled experiment: the metros differed in scale, location, corporate mix, institutions, and starting conditions. The comparison is useful because it shows how those differences affect the capacity to absorb a shock.

By 1970 Pittsburgh had become the third-largest corporate-headquarters city in the United States, with major firms across steel, aluminum, glass, petroleum, finance, engineering, and industrial equipment. 7 Universities, hospitals, banks, foundations, and professional firms provided channels that were related to—but not identical with—steel.

Bethlehem Steel’s namesake mill employed about 31,000 people during the 1960s while the city had roughly 75,000 residents. Not every worker lived in the city, but the figure conveys the corporation’s extraordinary influence over wages, land, taxes, identity, and civic life. 8

Pittsburgh’s institutions did not prevent suffering. Mill communities lost jobs, population, and wealth; recovery took decades and remained uneven. But Carnegie Tech, the Mellon Institute, the University of Pittsburgh, hospitals, foundations, banks, and corporate networks remained capable of receiving research money, training people, organizing projects, and supporting new industries. Carnegie Tech and the Mellon Institute merged in 1967 to form Carnegie Mellon University—an explicit conversion of earlier industrial wealth into a research institution. 9

Bethlehem Steel did reinvest. It built Burns Harbor, spent a record $674 million on capital expenditures in 1975, and began operating its first continuous caster that year. 11 The stronger criticism is not that the company made no investment. It is that much of the capital remained tied to the integrated-steel system, while newer foreign producers and domestic minimills adopted different technologies and cost structures. Continuous casting came comparatively late, and the firm carried growing pension and healthcare burdens. 10 12

The company’s collapse cannot be reduced to one villain. Management, labor relations, trade, technology, regulation, macroeconomic shocks, and legacy obligations all mattered. John Strohmeyer’s Crisis in Bethlehem is valuable precisely because it assigns responsibility broadly rather than turning the history into a simple shareholder-versus-worker story. 10

When the pension plans were transferred to the Pension Benefit Guaranty Corporation in 2002, they covered tens of thousands of workers and retirees and were underfunded by billions of dollars. 13 The city had fewer independent reservoirs ready to replace the corporate system at comparable scale.

Section 06

Glasgow and the Conversion of Surplus

Glasgow offers a boom-period version of the succession problem. Its eighteenth-century tobacco trade generated immense fortunes through Atlantic commerce built on enslaved labor and plantation production. Any account of the city’s capital formation must state that origin plainly.

Arthur Herman describes Glasgow firms that limited partner withdrawals and retained large portions of profit inside the business. A historical study by T. M. Devine independently records that Speirs, Bowman & Co. paid partners an annual 5 percent return on their shares in 1781, illustrating a structure that left substantial capital available to the firm. 14 15 This should not be generalized into a universal rule across every Glasgow merchant house.

The important mechanism is retained and redeployed surplus. Leading merchants invested beyond tobacco in banks, sugar, textiles, insurance, ironworks, printing, and other commercial ventures. Alexander Speirs, for example, held interests in banking, sugar, ironworks, and print fields. 16

When the American Revolution disrupted the tobacco trade, the diversified capital base did not eliminate losses. It did, however, give Glasgow more independent activities through which commercial knowledge, credit, and investment could continue operating. This is the same principle as Pittsburgh, but with a different institutional mechanism: not only philanthropy after wealth was created, but business structures that retained and diversified capital during the boom.

The lesson is not “trickle-down economics.” It is more specific: surplus becomes durable only when it is converted into capabilities and enterprises that can earn revenue independently of the original trade.

Section 07

One Hour Inside an Engineering Firm

Consider an engineering firm billing a client $300 for an hour of a mid-level engineer’s work. Assume, illustratively, that about $100 reaches the engineer as gross pay and $200 remains with the firm.

The firm’s share is not pure profit. It funds nonbillable staff, rent, software, insurance, accounting, business development, partner compensation, unpaid proposals, and project risk. The firm is itself a watershed: client revenue forks toward local payroll, distant software companies, insurers, landlords, lenders, owners, and vendors.

Now trace the worker’s $100. The essay’s illustrative allocation is $6 to retirement, $31 to federal, payroll, state, and local taxes, $21 to housing, $13 to groceries, and $29 to everything else. The exact household percentages vary. The useful question is where each channel goes next.

Section 08

From One Worker to One City

Multiply one worker’s transactions across every pay period, household, business, and institution. Repetition turns small routing differences into a regional pattern.

A region receives outside money through three broad channels: exported value sold to customers elsewhere; public distribution through contracts, grants, benefits, reimbursement, and infrastructure; and external private investment in plants, offices, data centers, housing, or industrial sites.

Micron: factory or ecosystem?

Micron plans to invest up to $100 billion over more than twenty years in as many as four semiconductor fabrication plants near Syracuse. The company projects about 9,000 direct jobs and nearly 50,000 total New York jobs. 30 Federal CHIPS incentives cover Micron projects in New York and Idaho, while New York has made up to $5.5 billion in Green CHIPS incentives available and established a $500 million community investment framework. 31 32

By July 2026, Micron reported its first concrete pour and said roughly $675 million—more than half of awarded early-site-work value—had gone to New York-based contractors and suppliers, with more than 80 percent of site workers from New York. These are early indicators, not proof of the final outcome. 33

Direct jobsWill the 9,000 positions materialize on schedule?
Supplier independenceCan regional suppliers serve customers besides Micron?
Transferable skillsWill training remain useful across semiconductor and advanced manufacturing employers?
Shared infrastructureCan power, water, transport, and housing investments support more than one company?
Research + startupsDo university partnerships create companies and intellectual property outside Micron?
Concentration riskDoes a diversification project become a new dominant-employer dependency?

The project is therefore not simply evidence for or against subsidies. It is a live test of institutional design. Central New York can receive a very large factory and still fail to build a semiconductor ecosystem. The decisive variables are supplier formation, transferable workforce capacity, shared infrastructure, enforceable commitments, and independent sources of future revenue.

What this section addsThe size of an investment matters less than whether it creates capabilities that can outlive the investor.

Section 09

Retirement Security Has Two Balance Sheets

Retirement policy usually focuses on the personal balance sheet: savings, Social Security, pensions, insurance, and home equity. That focus is necessary. At the end of March 2026, U.S. retirement assets totaled about $47.6 trillion, including approximately $9.9 trillion in 401(k) plans. 17

But retirement is lived inside a civic balance sheet. Can a person remain safely in a home? Reach food and medical care after driving becomes difficult? Maintain social connection and purpose? Obtain help before a small problem becomes a crisis?

The World Health Organization’s age-friendly framework includes housing, transportation, outdoor spaces, social participation, inclusion, communication, and community and health services. 18 The CDC reports that more than 14 million Americans age 65 and older—about one in four—report falling each year. 19 The National Institute on Aging links isolation and loneliness with higher risks of heart disease, depression, cognitive decline, and other health problems. 20

This reframes the local-investment question. The goal should not be to force retirees to accept lower returns for sentimental projects. Federal fiduciary rules prohibit ERISA managers from sacrificing return or taking greater risk merely to secure collateral benefits. 21

The serious design space is narrower and more credible: market-rate municipal bonds, revenue-producing accessible housing, transportation assets, healthcare facilities, professionally managed infrastructure funds, and optional participant-directed vehicles where expected risk and return remain competitive.

Two retirees with the same portfolio can experience different levels of security because one must privately purchase mobility, home modifications, social support, and emergency care that the other receives from a better-built environment. Financial wealth and civic infrastructure are complements.

Section 10

Healthcare as a Public Faucet

Healthcare is one of the main mechanisms pulling outside money into many post-industrial regions. Medicare, Medicaid, private insurance, and patient payments fund hospital payrolls, supplies, buildings, utilities, and professional services.

Medicare accounts for roughly one-quarter of hospital spending nationally and Medicaid for roughly one-fifth, although Medicaid is jointly financed by federal and state governments. 22 National health expenditures reached about $5.3 trillion in 2024, or 18 percent of GDP. 23

A hospital therefore imports money in a different way from a steel mill. It does not primarily sell a tradable product abroad; it serves local demand through national, state, employer, insurer, and household payment systems.

Ownership still matters, but the categories should be precise. Local payroll and building operations occur under both local and distant ownership. The difference appears in strategic control, borrowing, procurement rules, retained earnings, and the destination of residual margin.

Nonprofit ownership does not automatically guarantee local accountability, and for-profit ownership does not automatically erase local benefit. A locally anchored institution can still be inefficient; an outside operator can bring needed capital and expertise. The watershed question is where decisions and future surpluses are controlled after patient care is funded.

Section 11

The Construction Cash-Flow River

Construction turns a financing commitment into land, materials, equipment, wages, professional fees, and a completed asset. The flow is large, visible, and easy to misunderstand.

Developer return should not be stacked beside GC and subcontractor margins as though all are separate deductions from the same project budget. Contractor margins are embedded inside construction cost. Developer return is usually a residual outcome: what remains after the project is completed, financed, leased or sold, and debt is repaid.

Each participant is paid for a different exposure. Workers exchange labor for wages and generally do not participate in property-market upside. Subcontractors price scope and execution risk. General contractors coordinate many scopes, schedule, safety, and contractual risk. Developers bear entitlement, financing, market, and residual-value risk.

The hierarchy is not perfectly fair in every project, and bargaining power matters. The useful analytical rule is to ask what each margin is compensating and who bears the consequence when the underlying assumption fails.

What this section addsCash flow should be drawn without double-counting: project capital becomes costs and an asset; completed value determines residual developer outcomes.

Section 12

Who Chooses, Who Pays, and Who Bears the Cost?

Markets discipline a home renovation because the owner can see the quote, compare contractors, change scope, delay the work, inspect progress, and refuse future business. The feedback loop is short.

That loop weakens when the chooser, payer, beneficiary, and decision-maker are different people. A patient receives care while insurers, employers, taxpayers, and future premium payers absorb much of the cost. A student chooses a college using aid packages that can obscure net price and defer payment. A public project is selected, financed, designed, procured, built, operated, and used by different institutions and generations.

Decisions improve when the people making them share in both the gains and the losses. When they keep the upside and pass the downside to others, bad decisions become easier to repeat.

The price chart is not proof that weak accountability caused inflation. Technology made televisions and software dramatically better and cheaper. Global supply chains reduced some goods prices. Healthcare, education, and childcare are labor-intensive, regulated, quality-differentiated, and supply-constrained. Housing is tied to land and local rules. The chart is a prompt to examine the feedback system, not a complete causal model. 25 26

Higher education illustrates the ambiguity. Federal credit can expand access while also weakening immediate price sensitivity. A GAO review found widespread failures to provide clear net-price information, and research on loan-limit changes has found partial tuition pass-through, though estimates and interpretations differ. 27

Public infrastructure has formal oversight, yet responsibility can be fragmented across agencies, consultants, contractors, elected officials, funders, and operators. The NYU Transit Costs Project documents how governance, procurement, management, and institutional capacity contribute to unusually high U.S. transit costs. 28

The pizza shop and the short feedback loop

When I was sixteen, I worked at a small family-owned pizza shop. The father had started it and his son eventually took over. The owner knew regular customers by name. A bad experience was not an abstract metric: it threatened his livelihood and his family’s reputation in a community he could not easily exit.

That story belongs here because it demonstrates proximity. The person making decisions could see the customer, the payment, the result, and the next visit. Large systems need different tools—transparent prices, independent benchmarking, enforceable milestones, outcome tracking, audits, and clawbacks—to recreate some of that feedback at scale.

What this section addsWeak consumer feedback is not an argument against public funding; it is an argument for stronger, more visible accountability mechanisms.

Section 13

Exit, Voice, and the Loss of Capacity

Albert Hirschman described decline through the choices of exit, voice, and loyalty. People can stay and attempt to improve an organization or community, or leave and direct their effort elsewhere. The easier exit becomes for the most mobile and capable residents, the fewer people remain to organize change. 29

Leaving is often the rational and humane choice for the individual. I made it myself when I moved from Scranton to Boston. Boston is what decades of compounded inflow look like: universities, hospitals, research grants, engineering firms, construction, software, biotechnology, finance, and national institutions continuously attract one another.

No person experiences themselves as “the drain.” They experience a job, education, relationship, or opportunity. The regional effect appears only in aggregate: fewer entrepreneurs, mentors, professionals, donors, managers, volunteers, and future parents remain connected to the place.

Human capital therefore behaves like financial capital. Anchored people—through family, ownership, professional networks, civic duty, or genuine preference—are more likely to invest in long-term local capacity. But attachment cannot compensate indefinitely for a lack of viable work.

Section 14

Opportunity Is Sunlight

Sustained scarcity changes behavior before it changes character. In a shallow market, every large contract matters more. A disputed change order may determine whether a contractor makes payroll. A firm may protect today’s margin even if the tactic damages a relationship, because the next project is uncertain.

In a deeper market, future opportunities are visible. Reputation compounds. Cooperation on one difficult project can improve the probability of winning the next three. The same people, working under different opportunity conditions, can rationally behave differently.

The COLTS maintenance facility in Scranton helped clarify this for me. It was one of the region’s largest publicly funded projects in years, and disputes over scope, billing, and changes carried unusual pressure because comparable work was scarce. That single project cannot prove a universal rule, but it reveals the mechanism.

Boston construction is not free of conflict. The difference is market depth. Firms know that relationships may matter on the next hospital, university, laboratory, or infrastructure project already visible in the pipeline.

This avoids the lazy conclusion that one region simply has a “better culture.” Incentives, opportunity density, ownership, and repeated interaction shape behavior. Economic change cannot be reduced to attitude, but changing the flow can make more cooperative behavior rational.

Section 15

The Regional Succession Loop

The watershed metaphor is politically agnostic. Rain falls, and the shape of the land determines where it goes. Economic terrain is not natural, however. It is built through law, ownership, lending, education, infrastructure, procurement, taxation, philanthropy, and corporate governance.

The central thesis can now be stated precisely: a city’s long-term strength depends on converting temporary inflows into durable capabilities that can produce future inflows independently.

This framework also clarifies the limits of “buy local.” Local circulation can help, but circulation alone is not productivity. A dollar moving repeatedly through low-productivity transactions does not automatically create export capacity, technology, or durable wealth. Retention matters when it finances assets, skills, institutions, and firms capable of earning new outside revenue.

Likewise, outside ownership is not automatically extraction. A distant company can create valuable jobs, technology, tax revenue, and supplier demand. A local owner can mismanage a firm or capture public subsidy without delivering benefits. The relevant test is the total design: risk, accountability, tax capture, procurement, skills, ownership, and what remains.

Section 16

A Watershed in Progress

For northeastern Pennsylvania, the next test is already visible in land acquisition, zoning, power planning, gas infrastructure, logistics, advanced manufacturing, and data-center proposals. Luzerne County was considering a data-center zoning amendment in 2026. 34

The region has real structural advantages: industrial land, proximity to New York and Philadelphia, construction and technical labor, legacy infrastructure, and access to the Marcellus Shale. Susquehanna County is among Pennsylvania’s leading gas-producing counties. 35 The proposed Constitution Pipeline has also returned to federal proceedings, although its construction remains uncertain. 36

The failure mode is familiar: host a capital-intensive asset, absorb land, grid, water, noise, and infrastructure burdens, receive temporary construction work and limited permanent employment, and send most strategic value elsewhere.

Local ownership is one capture mechanism, but it is not the only one. Loudoun County demonstrates that absentee-owned data centers can still generate substantial local value when buildings and equipment are taxed effectively. County materials state that each dollar of county service to data centers produces many dollars of tax revenue and project revenues are expected to exceed $1 billion. 37 Virginia’s state sales-tax exemption simultaneously carries a large fiscal cost, demonstrating that local and state effects can point in different directions. 38

Throop offers a smaller example. Its 2026 budget expected $3.2 million from the Keystone landfill host-municipality fee plus a separate annual fee, against about $6.15 million in total anticipated revenue. 39 The facility carries environmental and quality-of-life costs; the point is that fiscal capture is visible and contractually structured.

Design the deal before the flow arrives

Tax and host-fee capture

Measure the public cost and secure durable revenue rather than relying on job announcements.

Transferable workforce

Train electrical, mechanical, controls, construction, and operations skills useful across employers.

Supplier formation

Help firms qualify for procurement while retaining the ability to serve other industries.

Shared infrastructure

Power, water, roads, fiber, and sites should support a regional platform—not only one tenant.

Independent institutions

Fund colleges, local lenders, research, and entrepreneurship organizations that survive the project.

Enforceable accountability

Tie public benefits to verified milestones, reporting, and clawbacks.

Johnson College has trained technical workers in Scranton since 1912. 40 Scaling institutions like it before demand fully arrives would follow the industrial-succession principle: build the pipe before the water, and make the skills useful beyond one employer.

Public investment can also crowd in private capacity. Research on defense R&D has found that higher public subsidies were followed by increased privately funded industry R&D, although local capture varies. 41 The same question applies to every infrastructure and industrial subsidy: does public money create an independent platform, or merely lower the cost of one private project?

The rain will fall. The shape of the land can still be decided.

What this section addsNortheastern Pennsylvania should evaluate each new project by the independent capabilities it leaves behind—not only by capital cost or construction jobs.
